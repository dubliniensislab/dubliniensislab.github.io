How pathogens cause disease and resist treatment

We are a microbiology research group at the Dublin Dental University Hospital. We study the fungal pathogen Candida albicans and antibiotic-resistant staphylococci: how they cause infection, what drives their virulence, and how they resist antimicrobial drugs.

Our name comes from Candida dubliniensis, the yeast species first described in this laboratory in 1995. Read our story →

    Our story · Ár scéal

    The story of the lab

    More than thirty years of microbiology research at the Dublin Dental University Hospital, from David Coleman's early work to the three groups of today.

    The labCandida biologyTLO genesAntibiotic-resistant bacteria
    1. 1989

      David Coleman joins the School of Dental Science

      Coleman, a Trinity graduate, joins the School of Dental Science and goes on to lead microbiology research at the Dublin Dental Hospital for more than three decades, later becoming Professor of Oral and Applied Microbiology and Head of the Division of Oral Biosciences.

    2. 1991

      Derek Sullivan joins the lab

      Derek Sullivan joins the microbiology group, beginning a long collaboration with David Coleman on the biology of Candida.

    3. 1995

      A new yeast species is named after Dublin

      Derek Sullivan, David Coleman and colleagues describe Candida dubliniensis, a new species found in the mouths of people with HIV infection. It gives the lab its name.

      Sullivan et al., Microbiology
    4. 2005

      Ten years of C. dubliniensis

      Sullivan, Moran and Coleman review a decade of work showing that, although it is very closely related to C. albicans, C. dubliniensis is less virulent, and that missing or divergent genes may explain the difference.

      Sullivan, Moran & Coleman, FEMS Microbiology Letters
    5. 2005+

      Cleaner water in the dental clinic

      Coleman's group develops automated systems that use electrochemically activated solutions to control biofilm in dental unit waterlines, work carried into infection control across the dental clinic.

    6. 2006+

      Tracking MRSA in Irish hospitals

      The lab builds a long-running programme on the population biology, evolution and antibiotic resistance of MRSA, moving to whole-genome sequencing to follow new clones as they emerge.

      e.g. Earls, Kinnevey et al. 2017, PLoS ONE
    7. 2014

      The TLO genes encode Mediator subunits

      Moran and Sullivan's groups show that the TLO genes of C. albicans encode subunits of Mediator that regulate distinct virulence traits, opening the lab's main line of Candida research today.

      Haran et al., PLoS Genetics
    8. 2018

      More TLO genes, more virulence

      Expansion of the TLO gene family is shown to enhance the virulence of Candida species.

      Flanagan et al., PLoS ONE
    9. 2023

      The whole TLO family deleted with CRISPR-Cas9

      Removing the whole family reveals different roles for the α, β and γ clades. Follow-up work links the family to antifungal tolerance (2024) and shows that α-TLO genes drive virulence (2026).

      Fletcher et al., PLoS Genetics
    10. 2025

      Hidden diversity of vancomycin-resistant enterococci

      Kinnevey, Coleman and colleagues find that 70% of patients studied in an Irish hospital carried more than one lineage of vancomycin-resistant E. faecium.

      Kavanagh, Kinnevey et al., JAC-Antimicrobial Resistance
    11. Today

      A new generation

      Following David Coleman's retirement, the lab continues as three research groups led by Gary Moran, Derek Sullivan and Peter Kinnevey, working on Candida gene regulation, fungal virulence and antibiotic-resistant bacteria.

    For patients, students and the public · Candida mínithe

    Candida explained

    A short guide to the microbes we study, what they do in the body, and why drug resistance matters.

    What is Candida?

    Candida is a group of yeasts, which are single-celled fungi. The most common species in people, Candida albicans, lives harmlessly in the mouth, gut and on the skin of many healthy people. Our immune system and the other microbes around it usually keep it in check.

    When does it cause disease?

    If that balance is upset, Candida can overgrow. In the mouth this causes oral thrush, often seen as creamy white patches, and it can cause inflammation under dentures. In people who are seriously ill in hospital, Candida can enter the bloodstream and cause a life-threatening infection.

    Who is most at risk?

    Oral thrush is more common in babies and older people, in denture wearers, in people with a dry mouth, diabetes or a weakened immune system, and after courses of antibiotics or steroid inhalers.

    How does it switch from harmless to harmful?

    C. albicans can change shape, from round yeast cells to long thread-like filaments called hyphae that can invade tissue. Much of our research asks which genes control this switch and how the fungus adapts to stress inside the body.

    Why drug resistance matters

    Antifungal drugs

    Only a few classes of antifungal drugs exist, including azoles such as fluconazole, echinocandins and polyenes such as nystatin and amphotericin B. When Candida becomes resistant or tolerant to them, infections are harder to treat. In 2022 the World Health Organization placed C. albicans in the critical group of its first list of priority fungal pathogens.

    Antibiotic-resistant bacteria

    Bacteria such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE) resist key antibiotics and spread in hospitals. We use whole-genome sequencing to follow how these bacteria move between patients and how they pick up resistance genes.

    Did you know?

    One Candida species is named after this city. Candida dubliniensis was first described at the Dublin Dental Hospital in 1995. Read the story of the lab →

    Research · Taighde

    Three questions about how microbes cause disease

    Our work spans fungal gene regulation, fungal virulence and bacterial genomics. Each theme is led by a principal investigator and builds on the lab's published work.

    Gene regulation
    Led by Derek Sullivan and Gary Moran
    α-TLOβ-TLOγ-TLO

    The TLO gene family and Mediator

    Candida albicans carries an unusually large family of TLO genes, most of them near the ends of its chromosomes. The exact number varies between strains and genome versions. They encode versions of Med2, a subunit of Mediator, the complex that links transcription factors to the machinery that switches genes on. The genes fall into three clades, α, β and γ.

    Using CRISPR-Cas9 to remove the whole family, the group has shown:

    • 2023
      The α, β and γ clades differ in how they regulate carbohydrate metabolism, stress responses, filamentous growth and virulence, with α-Tlo proteins acting as stronger activators.
    • 2024
      Losing the TLO family changes membrane sterol composition and increases tolerance to fluconazole.
    • 2025
      Fluconazole resistance driven by a hyperactive form of the transcription factor Tac1 depends on Tlo proteins.
    • 2026
      A single α-TLO gene is enough to restore full virulence to strains depleted of the family.
    α-cladeStronger transcriptional activators than the γ-clade. A single α-TLO gene restores full virulence to strains lacking the family.
    β-cladeA small clade, represented by TLOβ2 in the reference strain. A β-only strain keeps some wild-type traits.
    γ-cladeWeaker activators. A γ-only strain is impaired in filamentation, stress tolerance and virulence.

    Virulence
    Led by Gary Moran

    How C. albicans causes disease

    C. albicans lives harmlessly in most people's mouths but can cause infections ranging from oral thrush to life-threatening bloodstream disease. We use molecular genetics, genomics and transcriptomics to find out what makes it a successful pathogen: how it switches between yeast and filamentous forms, adapts to stress, and causes infection.

    Earlier comparative work with its close relative C. dubliniensis, which is much less often a cause of disease, helped identify traits that make C. albicans more pathogenic.

    Staphylococci
    Led by Peter Kinnevey

    Staphylococcal genomics and resistance

    Using whole-genome sequencing, we track the emergence of multidrug-resistant methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) in Irish hospitals, which informs how patients are screened.

    We also characterise the mobile genetic elements that staphylococci use to spread resistance and adapt to new niches, including the arginine catabolic mobile element (ACME) in Staphylococcus epidermidis from periodontal and peri-implant disease.

    Former head of the lab
    DC

    Prof. David Coleman

    Professor of Oral and Applied Microbiology (retired) · FTCD, MRIA, FRCPath

    David Coleman joined the School of Dental Science in 1989 and led the lab's microbiology research for more than three decades, serving as Head of the Division of Oral Biosciences. He co-discovered Candida dubliniensis in 1995, pioneered automated decontamination of dental unit waterlines, and built a long-running programme on the genomics and antibiotic resistance of MRSA and other hospital pathogens. He has published more than 240 peer-reviewed papers and received the Royal Irish Academy Silver Medal for Microbiology.
