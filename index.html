How pathogens cause disease and resist treatment

We are a microbiology research group at the Dublin Dental University Hospital. We study the fungal pathogen Candida albicans, the bacteria of the oral microbiome and antibiotic-resistant hospital pathogens: how they cause infection, what drives their virulence, and how they resist antimicrobial drugs.

Our name comes from Candida dubliniensis, the yeast species first described in this laboratory in 1995. Read our story →

      For patients, students and the public · Candida mínithe

      Candida explained

      A short guide to the microbes we study, what they do in the body, and why drug resistance matters.

      What is Candida?

      Candida is a group of yeasts, which are single-celled fungi. The most common species in people, Candida albicans, lives harmlessly in the mouth, gut and on the skin of many healthy people. Our immune system and the other microbes around it usually keep it in check.

      When does it cause disease?

      If that balance is upset, Candida can overgrow. In the mouth this causes oral thrush, often seen as creamy white patches, and it can cause inflammation under dentures. In people who are seriously ill in hospital, Candida can enter the bloodstream and cause a life-threatening infection.

      Who is most at risk?

      Oral thrush is more common in babies and older people, in denture wearers, in people with a dry mouth, diabetes or a weakened immune system, and after courses of antibiotics or steroid inhalers.

      How does it switch from harmless to harmful?

      C. albicans can change shape, from round yeast cells to long thread-like filaments called hyphae that can invade tissue. Much of our research asks which genes control this switch and how the fungus adapts to stress inside the body.

      Why drug resistance matters

      Antifungal drugs

      Only a few classes of antifungal drugs exist, including azoles such as fluconazole, echinocandins and polyenes such as nystatin and amphotericin B. When Candida becomes resistant or tolerant to them, infections are harder to treat. In 2022 the World Health Organization placed C. albicans in the critical group of its first list of priority fungal pathogens.

      Antibiotic-resistant bacteria

      Bacteria such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE) resist key antibiotics and spread in hospitals. We use whole-genome sequencing to follow how these bacteria move between patients and how they pick up resistance genes.

      Did you know?

      One Candida species is named after this city. Candida dubliniensis was first described at the Dublin Dental Hospital in 1995. Read the story of the lab →

      Gene regulation
      Led by Derek Sullivan and Gary Moran
      α-TLOβ-TLOγ-TLO

      The TLO gene family and Mediator

      Candida albicans carries an unusually large family of TLO genes, most of them near the ends of its chromosomes. The exact number varies between strains and genome versions. They encode versions of Med2, a subunit of Mediator, the complex that links transcription factors to the machinery that switches genes on. The genes fall into three clades, α, β and γ.

      Using CRISPR-Cas9 to remove the whole family, the group has shown:

      • 2023
        The α, β and γ clades differ in how they regulate carbohydrate metabolism, stress responses, filamentous growth and virulence, with α-Tlo proteins acting as stronger activators.
      • 2024
        Losing the TLO family changes membrane sterol composition and increases tolerance to fluconazole.
      • 2025
        Fluconazole resistance driven by a hyperactive form of the transcription factor Tac1 depends on Tlo proteins.
      • 2026
        A single α-TLO gene is enough to restore full virulence to strains depleted of the family.
      α-cladeStronger transcriptional activators than the γ-clade. A single α-TLO gene restores full virulence to strains lacking the family.
      β-cladeA small clade, represented by TLOβ2 in the reference strain. A β-only strain keeps some wild-type traits.
      γ-cladeWeaker activators. A γ-only strain is impaired in filamentation, stress tolerance and virulence.

      Oral microbiome
      Led by Gary Moran
      Oral Microbiome research group

      Oral microbes and disease

      The mouth is home to hundreds of species of bacteria and fungi. Most live there harmlessly, but some can cause disease when the balance of this community changes. The Oral Microbiome group studies the pathogenicity of oral microorganisms, including the fungal pathogen Candida albicans and the oral bacterium Fusobacterium nucleatum.

      We combine molecular genetics, genomics and transcriptomics to find out how these organisms adapt to the host and cause disease: for C. albicans, how it switches between yeast and filamentous forms, responds to stress and invades tissue. We also carry out metagenomics of the oral microbiome, sequencing the whole microbial community in patient samples, to better understand oral disease.

      Earlier comparative work with C. dubliniensis, a close relative that much less often causes disease, helped identify traits that make C. albicans more pathogenic.

      Staphylococci
      Led by Peter Kinnevey

      Staphylococcal genomics and resistance

      Using whole-genome sequencing, we track the emergence of multidrug-resistant methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) in Irish hospitals, which informs how patients are screened.

      We also characterise the mobile genetic elements that staphylococci use to spread resistance and adapt to new niches, including the arginine catabolic mobile element (ACME) in Staphylococcus epidermidis from periodontal and peri-implant disease.
